## 1. Problem and Dataset

### 1.1 Task Formulation

Large language models often give confident but wrong answers. In medical, legal, or financial settings, this hallucination problem is dangerous. Selective generation offers a principled fix: the model either answers or says "I don't know," and a statistical guarantee controls how often the shown answers are wrong.

We adopt the SGen framework of Lee et al. (2024) [[1]](#ref-lee2024selective). Given a question $x$, the model generates $G(x)$, and a selection function $\hat{S}(x)$ decides whether to show $G(x)$ or abstain. Correctness is judged by textual entailment rather than exact string match, because "Leonardo da Vinci painted it" and "the painter was Leonardo" should both count as right. The False Discovery Rate with respect to Entailment is the fraction of shown answers that fail to entail the reference:

$$\mathrm{FDR\text{-}E}(\hat{S}) \;=\; \Pr\{G(x) \notin E_{\mathrm{true}}(y) \mid \hat{S}(x) \neq \text{IDK}\}. \tag{1}$$

We want a PAC guarantee on this rate: $\Pr\{\mathrm{FDR\text{-}E}(\hat{S}) \leq \epsilon\} \geq 1 - \delta$. Throughout, we fix $\epsilon = 0.25$ and $\delta = 0.02$, matching the main experimental setting of Lee et al. (2024) [[1]](#ref-lee2024selective) and the defaults in our [`configs/default.yaml`](configs/default.yaml).[^1]

[^1]: All code, configuration files, and cached results for this project are available at <https://github.com/anshulk-cmu/DS-Gen-IML-10701>.

Here's the catch. Every bound in SGen assumes calibration and test data are drawn from the same distribution. Real deployments almost never satisfy this. When the distribution changes, the PAC bound can break silently and the user has no warning that it did. That is the gap this project sets out to close, and our experiments mark where the standard toolkit stops working.

### 1.2 Datasets

We work with two open-ended question answering datasets, both pulled from HuggingFace. TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) [[2]](#ref-joshi2017triviaqa) is our calibration source: `mandarjoshi/trivia_qa` with the `unfiltered.nocontext` configuration, validation split. We sample 3,610 questions (seed = 42) from the available 11,313 to match the size of our shifted test set. Natural Questions Open (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) [[3]](#ref-kwiatkowski2019natural) is the shifted test target: `google-research-datasets/nq_open`, validation split, all 3,610 questions.

These two datasets share a task (short open-ended QA against a reference answer) but differ in where the questions come from. TriviaQA draws from trivia websites; NQ draws from real Google search queries. That difference is precisely the domain shift we want to probe.

Answers are generated by GPT-4o-mini through the OpenAI API with the prompt "Answer the following question concisely in one sentence" and `max_tokens=512`. We collect one greedy answer with per-token log-probabilities (giving us $f_{M1}$, the mean log-probability) and $K=5$ temperature-0.7 samples (giving us $f_{M2}$, self-consistency). Correctness labels come from `microsoft/deberta-v2-xxlarge-mnli` running directional entailment from the greedy answer to the reference.

Under this pipeline, TriviaQA correctness is 70.8% and NQ correctness is 43.1%. That asymmetry drives most of the behavior we report later. One caveat worth stating up front: NQ's reference answers are short Wikipedia spans, and DeBERTa occasionally penalizes valid paraphrases. Both datasets feel this effect, so the 27.7-point gap reflects the model's actual knowledge, not the scorer.

### 1.3 PopQA for Screening

TriviaQA and NQ turned out to differ in more than just question source, so we added a second pair as a cleaner test of covariate shift alone. PopQA, introduced by Mallen et al. (2023) [[4]](#ref-mallen2023when) to probe long-tail factual knowledge, asks factual questions about Wikipedia entities, each tagged with a popularity score $s_{\mathrm{pop}}$. We split the test set on this score: the head is the top 60% by popularity, the tail is the bottom 20%. From each half we sample 1,000 questions, giving a run cost of roughly \$2 on the OpenAI API and a turnaround of about three minutes on one NVIDIA A6000.

Why this pair? Head and tail come from the same corpus, use the same question template, and are scored by the same pipeline. The only systematic difference is the popularity of the entity being asked about. If any benchmark should isolate covariate shift, it's this one.

What we found was less clean than we hoped. GPT-4o-mini gets 43.7% of head questions right under strict entailment and 24.1% of tail. That's a 19.6 point accuracy gap, and it rules out $\epsilon = 0.25$ on structural grounds: reaching that target needs 75% accuracy in the selected set, but the target pool sits at 24%. Popularity turns out not to be just a covariate. It is a proxy for whether the model saw the entity often enough during pretraining to remember it. That confound is the central lesson of our screening experiment, and we return to it in [Section 4](#4-experimental-results).

## 2. Related Works

### 2.1 Critical Reviews

**Paper A — Lee et al., "Selective Generation for Controllable Language Models," NeurIPS 2024 Spotlight** [[1]](#ref-lee2024selective).
The SGen framework of Lee et al. (2024) is the anchor reference for this project and the only prior work we know of that places a PAC-style guarantee on free-form language generation. Given a prompt $x$, SGen wraps an arbitrary generator $G$ and learns a selection function $\hat{S}$ that either emits $G(x)$ or abstains with "I don't know," with correctness judged by directional textual entailment rather than exact string match — a choice that sidesteps the well-documented brittleness of string-matched QA scoring on paraphrases such as "Leonardo painted it" versus "the painter was Leonardo." Their core algorithmic contribution, SGen-Semi, runs conformal pseudo-labeling on a small labeled split $Z_E$ and uses the resulting entailment set to cheaply label a much larger unlabeled split $Z_U$, exploiting the FDR-E decomposition $\mathrm{FDR\text{-}E} = \mathrm{FER} - \mathrm{FNER} + \mathrm{NER}$ (their Lemma 1) to turn one unobservable quantity into three separately-bounded ones. Each term is controlled by a Clopper–Pearson binomial tail bound, and a Bonferroni correction over the threshold grid $|H|$ caps the joint failure probability below $\delta$, yielding the PAC guarantee $\Pr\{\mathrm{FDR\text{-}E}(\hat{S}) \leq \epsilon\} \geq 1 - \delta$. Empirically, SGen-Semi reaches $\approx 73.3\%$ selection efficiency with mean FDR-E $0.159$ on GPT-3.5-Turbo at $\epsilon = 0.25$, essentially matching the fully-supervised ceiling of $0.754$ while using only 75% of the labels, and beats the exact-match baseline SGen-EM by nearly twenty efficiency points. The limitation that motivates DS-SGen is both explicit and fatal: every tail bound assumes i.i.d. draws from a single distribution, so the guarantee says nothing — and breaks empirically — the moment calibration and deployment data diverge. As the most recent paper (NeurIPS 2024 Spotlight) in our bibliography, it sits at the center of our related work.

**Paper B — Tibshirani, Barber, Candès, Ramdas, "Conformal Prediction Under Covariate Shift," NeurIPS 2019** [[5]](#ref-tibshirani2019conformal).
Tibshirani, Barber, Candès, and Ramdas (2019) is the theoretical bedrock for every shift-aware conformal method that followed, including ours. The central result: if $P_{\mathrm{test}}(Y \mid X) = P_{\mathrm{cal}}(Y \mid X)$ but $P_{\mathrm{test}}(X) \neq P_{\mathrm{cal}}(X)$ — the standard covariate-shift assumption — then ordinary split-conformal coverage collapses, but coverage is fully restored by replacing the unweighted empirical quantile with the weighted quantile $Q^{w}_{1-\alpha}$, where each calibration score $V_i$ is reweighted by $w(x_i) = dP_{\mathrm{test}}(x_i) / dP_{\mathrm{cal}}(x_i)$, the likelihood ratio between test and calibration covariates. The proof device is *weighted exchangeability* (their Lemma 3), which relaxes strict i.i.d. exchangeability to a weaker symmetry under which the test score's rank among calibration scores follows a weighted rather than uniform distribution, and the quantile argument still goes through. The second contribution our pipeline uses is the *classifier trick* (Remark 3): because weights enter the quantile only as ratios, any constant rescaling cancels, so one can train a logistic regression $d(x)$ discriminating calibration from test covariates and set $\hat{w}(x) = d(x)/(1 - d(x))$ — the odds ratio — without ever touching the unknown densities $dP_{\mathrm{test}}$ or $dP_{\mathrm{cal}}$. The paper validates this on the UCI Airfoil benchmark with exponential-tilt covariate shift: unweighted conformal drops from $\approx 90.2\%$ to $82.2\%$ coverage, while oracle-weighted conformal recovers $90.8\%$ and classifier-estimated weights stay close to nominal. Two limitations make this paper necessary but not sufficient for our setting. First, the guarantee is covariate-shift *only* — it is silent when $P(Y \mid X)$ also changes (concept shift). Second, it presumes weights are either known or estimable to high accuracy, and under severe shift the Kish effective sample size $n_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum w)^2 / \sum w^2$ collapses. Both failure modes surface cleanly in our TriviaQA $\to$ NQ results.

### 2.2 Synthesis Across Two Tracks

The six papers in our bibliography fall cleanly onto two tracks that, until DS-SGen, have developed in parallel without meeting. The first is the *selective-prediction-for-generation* track, comprising SGen [[1]](#ref-lee2024selective) and Conformal Factuality [[6]](#ref-mohri2024conformal). SGen delivers a PAC finite-sample bound $\Pr\{\mathrm{FDR\text{-}E} \leq \epsilon\} \geq 1-\delta$ on full-sentence answers and abstains below a learned score threshold; Conformal Factuality [[6]](#ref-mohri2024conformal) instead operates at the sub-claim level, producing a back-off algorithm that progressively drops individual claims until an entailment-based marginal-coverage target is met, reporting 80–90% correctness on biography and math QA. Both take directional entailment as the correctness oracle — sidestepping exact-match brittleness — but both rely on exchangeability, so neither survives distribution shift. The second track — *shift-aware conformal prediction* — starts with weighted CP [[5]](#ref-tibshirani2019conformal), which restores marginal coverage under pure covariate shift via the weighted quantile $Q^{w}_{1-\alpha}$ and the odds-ratio classifier trick. DS-CP [[7]](#ref-lin2025dscp) pushes this idea into LLMs specifically: rather than a global likelihood ratio, it locally reweights calibration scores by sentence-embedding proximity to each test prompt, beating unweighted CP on MMLU under distribution shift. Enhanced CP [[8]](#ref-cherian2024enhanced) attacks the *conditional* validity gap for LLMs: it boosts nonconformity scores to be group-conditional and trades a small amount of marginal coverage for meaningful conditional guarantees on topics where the raw score is unreliable. Subpopulation CP [[9]](#ref-wang2025subpopulation) generalizes this further by relaxing the requirement that group membership be known — it infers subpopulation structure from the data itself and maintains coverage under *unknown* subpopulation shifts, which is closer to the deployment reality of an LLM served to an open population. The gap is sharp: no existing work satisfies (i) PAC finite-sample bounds, (ii) open-ended generation (not multiple-choice), (iii) abstention, and (iv) shift robustness *simultaneously*. Track 1 has (i)–(iii) but misses (iv); Track 2 has (iv) but targets marginal coverage rather than FDR-E, and most of its evaluation is on multiple-choice classification rather than free-form QA. DS-SGen is engineered to close precisely this gap: we plug the embedding-based reweighting of Lin et al. (2025) [[7]](#ref-lin2025dscp) into the FDR-E pipeline of Lee et al. (2024) [[1]](#ref-lee2024selective), with the option to fall back on the conditional-boosting route of Cherian, Gibbs, and Candès (2024) [[8]](#ref-cherian2024enhanced) (their Corollary A.1) when density-ratio estimation becomes unstable. Our pre-flight screening protocol (§3.5) is in turn informed by the multi-calibration hierarchy of Wang et al. (2025) [[9]](#ref-wang2025subpopulation), which formalizes when classifier-estimated group weights are trustworthy enough to be relied on.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Shared Pipeline (Stages 1–3)

All three methods sit atop an identical, fully cached three-stage backbone, so any performance gap reported in [Section 4](#4-experimental-results) reflects the calibration logic (Stage 4) alone rather than generation or scoring noise.

**Stage 1 — Data loading** ([`ds_sgen/data_loading.py`](ds_sgen/data_loading.py)). HuggingFace `datasets` pulls NQ-Open (`google-research-datasets/nq_open`, validation, 3,610 questions) and TriviaQA (`mandarjoshi/trivia_qa`, `unfiltered.nocontext`, 11,313 questions downsampled to 3,610 via `shuffle(seed=42).select(range(3610))` for size parity). Both are normalized to `{idx, question, reference_answer, all_answers, dataset}`; for TriviaQA the primary answer is `answer["value"]` and aliases are deduplicated with `dict.fromkeys` to preserve order.

**Stage 2 — Generation** ([`ds_sgen/generate_responses.py`](ds_sgen/generate_responses.py)). GPT-4o-mini via the OpenAI Chat Completions API, system prompt *"Answer the following question concisely in one sentence."*, `max_tokens=512`. Each question triggers two calls: (i) a greedy completion (`temperature=0`, `logprobs=True`) yielding the answer plus per-token log-probabilities, from which $f_{M1} = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(t_i\mid t_{<i},x)$; and (ii) $K{=}5$ samples at `temperature=0.7`, consumed downstream by $f_{M2}$. An exponential-backoff wrapper handles the OpenAI 10K-requests/day rate limit.

**Stage 3 — Entailment scoring** ([`ds_sgen/entailment_scoring.py`](ds_sgen/entailment_scoring.py)). `microsoft/deberta-v2-xxlarge-mnli` (1.5B parameters, `fp16`, batch 64; label order `{0:CON, 1:NEU, 2:ENT}`, hence the hard-coded `ENTAILMENT_IDX=2` — the order differs from `cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-large` and silently swapping the two would invalidate every score). Two NLI passes are run: (a) *unidirectional* greedy→reference for correctness, exposing both the continuous `entail_score`$=P(\text{ENT})$ used for conformal pseudo-labeling in §3.2 and the binary `entail_label`$=\mathbf{1}[\arg\max=\text{ENT}]$ used for FDR-E evaluation in §4; and (b) *bidirectional* NLI on all $\binom{K}{2}=10$ unordered sample pairs, with
$$f_{M2} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{10}\sum_{i<j}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,\mathrm{NLI}(s_i\!\to\!s_j)=\text{ENT}\;\wedge\;\mathrm{NLI}(s_j\!\to\!s_i)=\text{ENT}\,\right]. \tag{2}$$
Each question incurs $1 + K(K-1) = 21$ NLI forward passes (75,810 per dataset); NQ takes 4.8 min on an L40S. Outputs are JSON-cached so §3.2–§3.4 consume identical $(f_{M1}, f_{M2}, \mathtt{entail\_score}, \mathtt{entail\_label})$ tuples per question.

### 3.2 Method 1: SGen-Semi Baseline

**Implementation:** [`ds_sgen/sgen_semi.py`](ds_sgen/sgen_semi.py) — a faithful reimplementation of Algorithm 2 of Lee et al. (2024) [[1]](#ref-lee2024selective), cross-checked against the authors' reference code at <https://github.com/ml-postech/selective-generation>. We calibrate on TQA (`sgen.cal_dataset: "tqa"`, chosen because the $f_{M1}$–correctness correlation is stronger there) and treat held-out NQ as the shifted target. Each of 500 random splits (seed base $=42$) runs:

1. **Partition.** TQA ($n=3{,}610$) → 70% calibration / 30% in-domain test (`cal_frac=0.70`); then the calibration pool → 75% $Z_U$ (unlabelled) / 25% $Z_E$ (labelled) (`zu_frac=0.75`), giving $|Z_U|=1{,}895$ and $|Z_E|=632$ per split.
2. **Conformal pseudo-labelling.** On the correct-answer subset of $Z_E$ only, compute $\tau_{\mathrm{CP}}=s_{(k)}$ with $k=\lceil (n_{\mathrm{cor}}+1)\varepsilon_e\rceil$, $\varepsilon_e=0.05$; this is the *low* $\varepsilon_e$-quantile, so $(1-\varepsilon_e)$ of truly-correct $Z_E$ answers satisfy $s\ge\tau_{\mathrm{CP}}$ ([sgen_semi.py:52](ds_sgen/sgen_semi.py#L52)). Pseudo-label each $r\in Z_U$ as $\mathbf{1}[\texttt{entail\_score}_r\ge\tau_{\mathrm{CP}}]$.
3. **Threshold search.** Build a 20-point percentile grid on $Z_U$ `fM1` (`selection_mode: "fm1_only"`, so $|H|=20$ rather than $20{\times}20$); pick $\hat\tau_1$ maximising selection efficiency $|Z_U^{\text{sel}}|/|Z_U|$ subject to a Clopper–Pearson upper bound $\le\varepsilon$ on the pseudo-label failure rate.

$$\mathrm{FDR\text{-}E} \;=\; \underbrace{\Pr(e{=}0,\,\hat e{=}1)}_{\mathrm{FER}} \;-\; \underbrace{\Pr(e{=}1,\,\hat e{=}0)}_{\mathrm{FNER}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Pr(\hat e{=}0)}_{\mathrm{NER}} \tag{2}$$

(Eq. 2 is Lemma 1 of Lee et al. (2024) [[1]](#ref-lee2024selective).)

$$\mathrm{CP\text{-}upper}(f,n;\alpha) \;=\; B^{-1}\!\bigl(1-\alpha;\,f+1,\,n-f\bigr), \qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{adj}} \;=\; (\delta-\delta_p)\,/\,|H| \tag{3}$$

$$\hat S(x) \;=\; \begin{cases} G(x) & f_{M1}(x)\ge\hat\tau_1 \\ \text{IDK} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{4}$$

with $\delta_p=10^{-5}$ the pseudo-labelling failure budget (`sgen.delta_p`) and $B^{-1}$ the inverse regularised Beta CDF ([sgen_semi.py:81](ds_sgen/sgen_semi.py#L81)). Because `fM1` is continuous, `np.unique` preserves all 20 percentile points, so $|H|=20$ and the Bonferroni level is $\alpha_{\mathrm{adj}}\approx 10^{-3}$. Across 500 splits, in-domain TQA reaches validity $1.000$ / mean efficiency $0.408$, matching Lee et al. within split noise, but shifted NQ validity **collapses to $0.124$** (mean FDR-E $0.302$, 87.6% of splits over the $\varepsilon$ cap). The trained $\hat\tau_1$ is replayed unchanged on NQ — the failure mode §3.3–§3.4 attack.

### 3.3 Method 2: Conservative Threshold

**Implementation:** [`ds_sgen/conservative.py`](ds_sgen/conservative.py) — three naive domain-shift "fixes," each a one-line modification to Method 1's grid-search loop. All three share the Method 1 pseudo-labelling path; only the grid constraint or the winning $\hat\tau_1$ is perturbed. **Validity is always evaluated against the original** $\varepsilon=0.25$ ([conservative.py:237](ds_sgen/conservative.py#L237)), so the three options can be compared on one scale.

- **Option A — Safety factor on $\hat\tau_1$.** After standard grid search, inflate the winner: $\hat\tau_1\leftarrow\hat\tau_1+\log\gamma$ (since $f_{M1}$ is a log-probability, this multiplies the implied probability cutoff by $\gamma$); swept $\gamma\in\{1.0,1.2,1.5,2.0\}$ ([conservative.py:211](ds_sgen/conservative.py#L211)). The `tau2 *= gamma` branch at [line 213](ds_sgen/conservative.py#L213) is dead code under `fm1_only`.
- **Option B — Reduced $\varepsilon$ in the grid constraint.** Replace the CP cap by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}=\varepsilon/k$, $k\in\{1.0,1.5,2.0,3.0,4.0\}$ ([line 186](ds_sgen/conservative.py#L186)). Because evaluation uses the original $\varepsilon$, not $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$, this sweep quantifies how much stricter calibration must be to survive shift.
- **Option C — Delta budget allocation.** Reserve $\delta_s=\mathrm{frac}\cdot(\delta-\delta_p)$, giving $\delta_{\mathrm{cp}}=\delta-\delta_p-\delta_s$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{adj}}=\delta_{\mathrm{cp}}/|H|$; frac $\in\{0,0.25,0.50,0.75\}$ ([line 160](ds_sgen/conservative.py#L160)). A smaller $\alpha_{\mathrm{adj}}$ widens every Clopper–Pearson bound uniformly, tightening selection at every grid point.

**Empirical behaviour (NQ shifted).** Options A and B behave as on/off switches: at the identity setting both recover Method 1 ($12.4\%$ validity, $22.9\%$ efficiency); at the first non-trivial setting ($\gamma\ge 1.2$, $k\ge 1.5$) efficiency crashes to $0.000$ and validity trivially reaches $1.000$. Option C alone moves validity *continuously*: $12.4\to 13.8\to 15.4\to 22.0\%$ as frac goes $0\to 0.75$, while efficiency degrades only from $22.9\%$ to $18.5\%$. No sweep reaches the $98\%$ PAC target; the best (Option C, frac $=0.75$) still falls $76$ points short — the gap Method 3 sets out to close.

### 3.4 Method 3: DS-SGen (Proposed)

**Implementation:** [`ds_sgen/importance_weighted.py`](ds_sgen/importance_weighted.py). Our proposed method plugs the density-ratio reweighting of Lin et al. (2025) [[7]](#ref-lin2025dscp) — grounded in Tibshirani et al. (2019) [[5]](#ref-tibshirani2019conformal) — into the SGen-Semi pipeline of §3.2. Five steps:

1. **Embed** all $7{,}220$ questions (3,610 TQA + 3,610 NQ) with `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` → 384-dim `float32`, cached to `{cal,shifted}_embeddings.npy`.
2. **Domain classifier.** `LogisticRegression(C=1.0, solver="lbfgs", max_iter=1000, penalty="l2")` with TQA$\to 0$, NQ$\to 1$; 5-fold stratified CV accuracy **$=0.917$**, meaning the two distributions are nearly linearly separable — already a warning sign that the classifier trick will produce heavy weights.
3. **Weights.** For each TQA sample: clip $\hat p(x)\in[0.01,0.99]$, form $\hat w_i=\hat p_i/(1-\hat p_i)$, cap at the 95-th percentile (`weight_clip_percentile: 95`), normalise so $\sum_i\hat w_i=n$ ([importance_weighted.py:111–124](ds_sgen/importance_weighted.py#L111-L124)). Raw $\hat w_{\max}=32.66$, post-clipping $\hat w_{\max}=5.69$ — the cap binds hard.
4. **Weighted conformal threshold** on the correct-answer subset of $Z_E$: the smallest $q$ with $\sum_i \hat w_i\mathbf{1}\{s_i\le q\}\,/\,\sum_i\hat w_i\ \ge\ \varepsilon_e$ ([line 169](ds_sgen/importance_weighted.py#L169)).
5. **Weighted Clopper–Pearson.** At each grid point compute the weighted failure rate and Kish $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ **of the selected subset** (not of all $Z_U$), then apply CP with fractional counts $f_{\mathrm{eff}},\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}-f_{\mathrm{eff}}$; if $n_{\mathrm{eff}}<5$ the bound returns $1$ (vacuous) ([line 197](ds_sgen/importance_weighted.py#L197)).

$$\hat w(x) \;=\; \frac{\hat p(x)}{1-\hat p(x)} \;\propto\; \frac{P_{\mathrm{target}}(x)}{P_{\mathrm{cal}}(x)} \tag{5}$$

(Eq. 5 is the classifier trick of Tibshirani et al. (2019) [[5]](#ref-tibshirani2019conformal), Remark 3.)

$$n_{\mathrm{eff}} \;=\; \frac{\bigl(\sum_i\hat w_i\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_i\hat w_i^{\,2}} \qquad \text{(Kish design effect)} \tag{6}$$

$$\mathrm{CP\text{-}upper}^{w}(f_{\mathrm{eff}}, n_{\mathrm{eff}};\alpha) \;=\; B^{-1}\!\bigl(1-\alpha;\,f_{\mathrm{eff}}+1,\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}-f_{\mathrm{eff}}\bigr). \tag{7}$$

**Diagnostics & critique.** Full-$Z_U$ $n_{\mathrm{eff}}/n=1{,}113/3{,}610=30.8\%$; mean per-split $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=584$ — the 91.7% classifier combined with the 95-th-percentile clip leaves roughly one-third of the nominal calibration mass usable. Crucially, test-set FDR-E is measured **unweighted** ([line 395](ds_sgen/importance_weighted.py#L395)) — reweighting enters only through calibration, never through evaluation. On shifted NQ, Method 3 lifts validity from $12.4\%$ (M1) to $68.8\%$, but selection efficiency collapses from $22.9\%$ to $8.1\%$. Most of the validity gain is therefore *mechanical abstention*, not a genuine tightening of the PAC bound — a caveat §4.5 picks apart.
